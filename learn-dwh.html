What is a Data Warehouse (DWH)?

A library for your data - sorted, indexed, and built for asking questions, not for running your app.

The 30-second answer

A data warehouse is a database designed for analysis, not for running your app. Your application database (Postgres, MySQL, MongoDB) is built for fast inserts and updates - adding orders, recording logins, the operational side. A warehouse is built for fast reads across millions of rows - answering questions like "what was revenue by channel by week, last quarter?"

Same data, different job.

Why you can't just query your app database

Three reasons most teams hit a wall:

  1. Performance. Heavy analytical queries lock tables and slow down your live app. Customers feel it.
  2. Joining sources. Your CRM is in HubSpot, your orders are in Postgres, your ads are in Meta. They never meet - until you put them in one warehouse.
  3. History. Your app database stores the current state. Warehouses store every change, so you can ask "what was this customer's plan six months ago?"

The four layers inside a warehouse

  1. Raw. Exact copy of source data, untouched. The "audit trail" - you can always go back.
  2. Staging. Cleaned and lightly modeled. Renamed columns, fixed types, deduped rows.
  3. Marts. Business-friendly tables grouped by domain - marketing.attribution, finance.revenue, product.activation. This is what dashboards read.
  4. Metrics layer. Definitions of "revenue", "MRR", "active user" - defined once, used everywhere.

The big players

  • BigQuery (Google). Serverless, pay-per-query. Best for teams already on GCP.
  • Snowflake. Multi-cloud, separates storage from compute. Great for teams that need fine-grained scaling.
  • Redshift (AWS). Mature, AWS-native, predictable pricing.
  • Databricks. If your data team is heavily ML / Python.

For most teams under 50 people, BigQuery wins on simplicity and cost. Above that, the choice is more about ecosystem fit than features.

Common pitfalls

  • Skipping staging. Going from raw → dashboards directly. Works for a month, breaks the moment a source schema changes.
  • No naming convention. revenue_v2_FINAL_use_this_one ends up in production. We've all seen it.
  • Over-engineering early. You don't need a real-time streaming pipeline on day one. Start with hourly batches and add streaming when a real use case demands it.
  • No tests. The first time a column starts returning nulls silently, you'll wish you had dbt tests in place.

What good looks like

  • Source data lands in raw within an hour of the event.
  • A new dashboard takes a day to build, not a week.
  • Cost is predictable and tracked per team or per dashboard.
  • Every metric has a definition, an owner, and a test.
  • When something breaks, you know first - not your CEO.
